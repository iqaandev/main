You can tell if a business model works before spending a year on it. The math fits on a napkin.

Two numbers. What a customer is worth over their lifetime, divided by what they cost you to acquire. LTV divided by CAC.

Lifetime value is monthly revenue times gross margin, divided by monthly churn. Margin matters because serving the customer costs money. Churn matters because it's the clock. Acquisition cost is everything you spent to win them, fully loaded. The founders doing sales themselves like to forget that part.

Quick example. A product at $50 a month, 80% margin, 3% monthly churn. LTV is 50 times 0.8, divided by 0.03. Roughly $1,333. If each customer costs $300 to acquire, the ratio is about 4.4.

Now the zones, and why people memorize them:

Under 1, every new customer makes the hole deeper. You're buying revenue at a loss.
Between 1 and 3, it's fragile. Fine while things improve, dangerous the moment they don't.
Above 3 is the healthy zone most investors look for.
Above 5, and you might actually be underinvesting in growth.

Here's the part that surprises people. Take that $50 product and let churn slip from 3% to 5%. Lifetime value drops from $1,333 to $800. The ratio falls from 4.4 to 2.7, healthy to watch-list, and nothing about the product or price changed.

Retention work is unit economics work. Churn is a multiplier on everything else.

One afternoon with your real numbers and a spreadsheet. Worth doing before anyone asks you for these figures and you have to guess.
