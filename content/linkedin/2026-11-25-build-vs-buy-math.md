# Post 7, this week (worked math, English)

A team I know spent $30,000 building something they could subscribe to for $79 a month. They were right to build it. The math that told them so takes one post.

The comparison everyone does is subscription price versus build quote. $79 a month versus $30,000. Buy wins, meeting over, everyone feels efficient.

That math is wrong because it compares one seat and one year against forever. Here's the honest version, three lines.

Line one, the subscription over three years, all seats. That $79 tool is per person. A 20 person company pays $56,880 over 36 months. A 5 person company pays $14,220. Same tool, same quote, four times the commitment. Seat count is the whole game and almost nobody multiplies it out.

Line two, the build over three years, all costs. The $30,000 gets you to launch. Then staying alive costs 15 to 20 percent of the build per year, every year, which we've covered before. Call it $43,500 all-in over the same 36 months. Plus your team's hours choosing, reviewing, deciding. Those are real too, they just hide in salaries.

Line three, the bend tax on buying. Off-the-shelf never quite fits, so your team works around the gaps. Three hours a week of workarounds across a few people is north of $20,000 a year in loaded cost. This line is the one buyers forget and vendors never mention.

Put together, the rule of thumb falls out on its own.

If three years of seats costs less than a third of the build, buy it. No meeting needed, the gap is too wide.

If three years of seats costs more than the build, build it. Same reason, other direction.

Between those two, that's where the thinking lives. What's your edge, how bad is the bend, how long will you keep it.

For the record, that team of 20 did the multiplication, saw $57k of subscriptions against $43k of build, and chose to own the thing their work ran on. A 4 person team with the same quote should buy the tool and never think about it again.
