# Post 5, this week (contrarian take, English)

**Tip:** Tue-Thu 8-10am, personal profile. Attach `images/post5-fixed-price.png`. No link needed, or the cost article in FIRST COMMENT phrased as "wrote more on this".

---

Every fixed-price software contract contains a lie. Usually two, and everyone in the room knows it.

Lie number one comes from the studio side. To quote one number for six months of unknowable work, the price has to include padding for everything that might go wrong. You end up paying for risks that never happen. It isn't really an estimate, it's insurance, and you're the customer.

Lie number two comes from the client side. To keep that number acceptable, the scope gets quietly shrunk in the spec. The thing you actually wanted lives in the gaps between requirements, waiting to become a change request.

Then the project starts, reality shows up, and the change orders begin. That's the real billing. I've seen the "fixed" part of a fixed-price project end up being about 60 percent of what the client eventually paid. The other 40 percent arrived one small addition at a time.

Is this greed on one side and sneakiness on the other? Mostly no. Both sides are responding rationally to a bad instrument. Fixed price forces everyone to bet against uncertainty, and the house always builds in its edge.

What works better isn't exotic. A short paid discovery to shrink the unknowns. Then a range with the assumptions written down, and a sane rate for the changes that were always coming. Feels less safe on signing day. Is safer by month three.

If a contract's job is to help two strangers trust each other, fixed price quietly does the opposite.

What's your change-order horror story? Give me the percentage of the original budget, if you dare.
